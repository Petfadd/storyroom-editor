DOCUMENTARY PRODUCTION SCRIPT — REUSABLE TEMPLATE (Beat-based)

WHAT THIS IS
This is the working format for a documentary whose picture is chosen beat by beat from
your own footage, with narration over it and the real spoken moments left in. It is the
format the storyboard reads directly, so no separate block map or conversion step is
needed. It follows the structure that worked on the finished project "qqqqqqqq", with the faults
that project still carried closed: two of its beats asked for footage past the end of the
file they named, and its picture needed reusing the same shot. Both are now caught before
a build instead of during one.

Read the RULES section once, then fill in the TWO PARTS. Delete every line in square
brackets; they are instructions, not script.

The templates, in the order you will need them:

  A — a narration beat                        D — a second bite at the same pause
  B — a sound bite                            E — a sound-bite hook that opens the film
  C — narration that resumes after bites      F — a photograph of a person, on screen
                                                  while their name is said, and again
                                                  every time the name comes back

================================================================================
PART 1 — THE NARRATION TABLE (this becomes your SRT)
================================================================================

The storyboard needs an SRT. You can upload an .srt file, or you can let the script
provide one: a table shaped like the one below is read automatically, and its wording,
order and timing are copied, never rewritten. Use exactly these three columns.

Narration blocks:

| Block | Narration timeline | Wording |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 00:00:00,000 → 00:00:04,000 | [the exact words spoken in this block] |
| 2 | 00:00:04,000 → 00:00:09,400 | [the exact words spoken in this block] |
| 3 | 00:00:09,400 → 00:00:15,000 | [the exact words spoken in this block] |
| 4 | 00:00:15,000 → 00:00:21,750 | [the exact words spoken in this block] |

Table rules — each one is checked:

1. Every block's start is the previous block's end. No holes, no overlaps. A few
   milliseconds of rounding is snapped and recorded; anything larger refuses the table.
2. Block numbers run 1, 2, 3 … with none skipped.
3. Block 1 starts at 00:00:00,000.
4. The last block's end equals the duration of your narration audio, to within a quarter
   of a second. This is the most common mistake: the table stops short of the audio and
   the closing words have no block.
5. Wording is what is actually spoken — one block, one stretch of speech. Do not number
   them Srt001/Srt002; plain numbers are what the table reader expects.

If the SRT already exists separately, upload it and leave this table out. If you use both,
the uploaded .srt wins and the table is ignored.

TEMPLATE E — a sound-bite hook, before any narration
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A film may open on one to three bites with no narration at all. That is the hook: the
first thing the viewer hears is a person speaking, not a narrator explaining. Begin at
Master 00:00:00,000 and put the first narration beat at the master mark where the last
hook bite ends. No narration clock exists yet, so a bite that opens the film is anchored
at 00:00:00,000 and does not name a pause.

Beat: 001
Type: DOCUMENTARY SOUND BITE
Master timeline: 00:00:00,000 --> 00:00:11,000 (Duration: 11 sec)
Narration clock: PAUSED at 00:00:00,000
Narration state: PAUSED (Suspended for duration of bite)
Speaker: [the person on screen — and they must BE on screen for this range]
Exact spoken dialogue: "[the line that stops a viewer scrolling]"
Full source: [exact uploaded filename, with extension]
Source in: 00:00:03,500
Source out: 00:00:14,500
Footage audio: UNMUTED (100% Dialogue Level, Clean)
Cut action: HARD CUT TO SPEAKER
Visual direction: [their face, one unbroken take, live footage — never a station card]
On-screen caption: [their name and role, or None]
Continuity note: Opens the film. Narration begins at master 00:00:11,000.

Beat: 002
Type: DOCUMENTARY SOUND BITE
Master timeline: 00:00:11,000 --> 00:00:23,000 (Duration: 12 sec)
Narration clock: PAUSED at 00:00:00,000
Narration state: PAUSED (Suspended for duration of bite)
Speaker: [a second voice, if the hook needs one]
Exact spoken dialogue: "[the counterweight or the detail that raises the question]"
Full source: [exact uploaded filename, with extension]
Source in: [a range where THIS speaker is visible]
Source out: [inside the same file's live picture]
Footage audio: UNMUTED (100% Dialogue Level, Clean)
Cut action: HARD CUT TO SPEAKER
Visual direction: [the second face]
On-screen caption: [their name and role, or None]
Continuity note: Second hook bite, same pause point. Narration starts after it.

Beat: 003
Type: VOICE OVER
Master timeline: 00:00:23,000 --> 00:00:41,000
Narration clock: 00:00:00,000 --> 00:00:18,000
Narration state: PLAYING
Spoken narration: "[the narration picks up from the very start of the audio file]"
Full source: [exact uploaded filename, with extension]
Source in: 00:00:00,000
Source out: 00:00:18,000
Footage audio: MUTED (0%)
Cut action: CUT TO [the new picture]
Visual direction: [what the picture must show here]
On-screen caption: None
Continuity note: First narration. Its narration clock starts at 0 because nothing was
  consumed before it; the hook bites do not advance the narration clock.

Two rules for a hook:
  - Every hook bite must have its speaker visible for its whole range. A hook on a
    station graphic is the worst possible opening.
  - Keep it short. Two bites and about twenty seconds is a hook; four bites is an
    introduction, and the viewer has already decided.

Three bites work exactly the same way: give every one of them ALL of these three lines,
because consecutive bites must share one pause point and that shared point is what
anchors them at the start of the film —

    Narration clock: PAUSED at 00:00:00,000
    Narration state: PAUSED (Suspended for duration of bite)
    Master timeline: [each starts where the one before it ended]

A common way to get this wrong is to give bite two a pause time equal to bite one's
master OUT. That makes it a bite *after* narration that does not exist, and the build
refuses it. All three hook bites are paused at 00:00:00,000; only their Master timeline
moves forward.

TEMPLATE F — a photograph of a person, and the same photograph again later
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A downloaded picture of a victim, a suspect, a family member or anyone else strong in
the case belongs on screen while the narrator says their name — and it should come back
every time the name comes back.

A Beat cannot hold a Photograph. The Beat reader requires a video filename and a source
range, and the documentary renderer, frame audit and preview all refuse anything that is
not video. The way to put a still on screen is to turn the photograph into a short,
silent, motionless video once, and then name that video in the beats that mention the
person. The picture is identical in every frame, so what the viewer sees is the still.

STEP 1 — make one hold clip per person. In Command Prompt, from the app folder:

    .venv\Scripts\python tools\photo_to_clip.py photos --out clips --seconds 90

The older Python on this machine is fine too:

    python tools\photo_to_clip.py photos --out clips --seconds 90

`photos` is a folder of downloads (JPG, PNG or WebP). Each becomes
`<name>_hold.mp4` in `clips` — silent, 29.97 fps, 1920x1080, with the picture never
stretched and never cropped. A portrait or a 4:3 mugshot is filled out with a blurred,
darkened copy of itself, which is how a still is normally framed on television. Use
`--background black` if this production already matches the plain letterbox look.
Ninety seconds is about half a megabyte, because every frame is the same picture, and
it is enough for roughly thirty separate mentions of one person. FFmpeg is already
inside this app; never install one.

STEP 2 — upload the hold clips with your footage, exactly as you upload the MP4s.

STEP 3 — in every beat that names the person, point at that one clip and take a
different, non-overlapping source window for each mention. The hold is silent, so the
narration beat's own rule applies unchanged: ordinary footage is MUTED.

Beat: 012
Type: VOICE OVER
Master timeline: 00:00:41,000 --> 00:00:44,500
Narration clock: 00:00:18,000 --> 00:00:21,500
Narration state: PLAYING
Spoken narration: "her daughter, Maliyah Bass, was two years old"
Full source: Maliyah_Bass_hold.mp4
Source in: 00:00:00,000
Source out: 00:00:03,500
Footage audio: MUTED (0%)
Cut action: CUT TO THE PHOTOGRAPH
Visual direction: Maliyah Bass's photograph, held still. Her face fills the screen while
  the narrator says her name.
On-screen caption: Maliyah Bass, 2
Continuity note: First mention of the child. The photograph returns at 00:01:52,000.

Beat: 027
Type: VOICE OVER
Master timeline: 00:01:52,000 --> 00:01:55,000
Narration clock: 00:01:29,000 --> 00:01:32,000
Narration state: PLAYING
Spoken narration: "Maliyah Bass had been missing for two days"
Full source: Maliyah_Bass_hold.mp4
Source in: 00:00:20,000
Source out: 00:00:23,000
Footage audio: MUTED (0%)
Cut action: CUT BACK TO THE PHOTOGRAPH
Visual direction: The same photograph again, held still.
On-screen caption: Maliyah Bass, 2
Continuity note: Second mention. Same file, a different window, so the build accepts it.

Three rules for photographs:
  - A different window for every mention. Two beats may not reuse the same source
    range on one file: that is an unapproved overlap and the build refuses it. Separate
    windows need no approval, and since every frame of a hold is the same picture, the
    viewer sees the same photograph each time.
  - Keep each hold on screen about 2–4 seconds. Long enough to read a face, short enough
    that the film keeps moving. The 10% stills limit is generous — an eighteen-minute
    film allows almost two minutes of photographs — but it is enforced, and the app will
    name the beats that break it.
  - A caption with the name and role stays on the photograph. When the narrator says a
    name and the picture is on screen, the caption is what tells a viewer who they are
    looking at.

What a photograph cannot do: it cannot be the picture for a sound bite. A bite is a
person speaking; showing a still while they speak breaks the rule that whoever is
talking must be visible. Photographs go on narration, and only narration.

================================================================================
RULES — READ THIS ONCE, THEN KEEP TO IT
================================================================================

THE ONE RULE THAT BREAKS EVERYTHING ELSE
Every Source range must fit inside the file it names. If your script asks for footage
past the end of a video, the storyboard cannot be built, and no amount of review will fix
it. Before you upload, check each beat: Source out must be less than or equal to the
length of that file. The app now reports this on upload — but the fix has to happen in
your script.

THE NARRATION CLOCK IS A SINGLE CONTINUOUS CLOCK
It starts at 00:00:00,000 and it never skips while a bite plays — it is paused, not
advancing. Write it like this:

  - first narration beat:  Narration clock: 00:00:00,000 --> 00:00:33,600
  - the bite:              Narration clock: PAUSED at 00:00:33,600
  - the next narration:    Narration clock: RESUMES at 00:00:33,600 --> 00:02:27,166

So every RESUMES begins with the exact time the PAUSED line named. The final narration
OUT equals your narration file's duration. Missing that is what leaves the closing words
with no beat.

THE MASTER CLOCK IS THE FINISHED DOCUMENTARY CLOCK
It runs from 00:00:00,000 to the total runtime and it does not pause. A narration beat
takes up exactly the narration it covers. A bite takes up its own duration. So each beat
starts where the previous one ended.

Your TOTAL MASTER RUNTIME is therefore arithmetic, not a wish:

  TOTAL MASTER RUNTIME = length of the narration audio + duration of every bite

Write the number that comes out of that sum. A header that says 550 seconds on a film
whose bites alone run 749 seconds is not a rounding error, and it is not a small mistake:
it means the master clock cannot match the picture anywhere in the second half. Measured
on the project this template came from, the stated runtime was 550s and the real sum was
1117.6s — the hook had been designed for a 9-minute film that turned out to be 18.

Bites are the expensive part of the runtime, and they are the part people forget to add
up. Total them first, then plan the narration to fit the piece you actually want.

Picture length must equal timeline length. For a narration beat:
  (Narration out − Narration in) = (Source out − Source in) = (Master out − Master in)
For a bite:
  (Source out − Source in) = Duration
  (Master out − Master in) = Duration

THE DURATION FIELD ON A BITE
Write it on the Master timeline line as shown, either as `(Duration: 32.5 sec)` or
`(Duration: 1 min 06 sec)`, or on its own line as `Duration: 00:00:32,500`. Whichever you
use, it must equal Source out minus Source in exactly. If it does not, the beat is
refused.

TIMECODES
Always HH:MM:SS,mmm — `00:01:15,600`. Two digits for hours, minutes and seconds, exactly
three for milliseconds. A missing column or a dot instead of a comma will stop the read.

FILENAMES
Use the exact uploaded name, with extension, on one line, with no path and no folder:
`Full source: S01.mp4`. Never `./S01.mp4`, `footage/S01.mp4` or a wildcard. If the name
does not match an uploaded file exactly, the beat is refused, by design.

THE FIVE FIELDS THAT MUST BE EXACTLY RIGHT
  Type                VOICE OVER  or  DOCUMENTARY SOUND BITE
                      (NARRATION (VOICE OVER) is also accepted)
  Narration state     PLAYING / RESUMING on narration; PAUSED on a bite
  Footage audio       MUTED (0%) on narration; UNMUTED (…) on a bite
  Source in / out     inside the real file, with the right span
  Narration clock     continuous, as described above

ONE FIELD, ONE LINE
Do not write any field twice in the same beat — two Source in lines is a hard error, not a
last-one-wins. Blank lines and `--------` separators between beats are fine and ignored.

EVERY BEAT IS A SINGLE BLOCK
Begin with `Beat: 001`, then the fields, then the next `Beat: 002`. Number them in order
with no duplicates and no gaps, and nothing else may start a line with `Beat:` — that word
begins a new beat. Other notes are safe: `Continuity note:` or `Note:` are read as
editorial text and ignored by the checks.

ORDER OF BEATS
Either kind of beat may open or close the film, so a documentary can start on real speech
before the first line of narration, and end on a bite instead of a voice-over. A bite with
no narration before it is anchored at 00:00:00,000; a closing bite starts at the end of
your narration. Inside the film a bite must follow narration, or another bite at the same
pause time. Two bites in a row are allowed only when they share the same pause time, which
is how a two-voice exchange is written (Template D).

A NOTE ON THE NARRATION CLOCK BETWEEN BEATS
The narration clock is the clock of your narration file, not of the finished film. When a
bite interrupts narration, the next narration beat begins a few hundred milliseconds
after the previous one ended, because that is where the speech actually resumes. Those
hundredths are not an error and not a hole in your planning: they are the pauses you
left for the bites, and the bites cover them on the finished timeline. Do not close them
up by hand. What the script must get right is that every bite's PAUSED time equals the
narration OUT immediately before it, and that the final narration OUT equals your audio
duration.

THE PICTURE MUST STILL BE LIVE WHERE YOU QUOTE SOMEONE
Every arithmetic rule above can pass and the film still fail, because the numbers are
about *time*, not about *what is on screen*. The commonest way a broadcast recording
breaks a script is a house card: a station ident, "click to subscribe", an app promo.
The recorder keeps capturing sound while the picture cuts away, so a speaker you quote
from that stretch has a correct timecode and a correct duration, and the reader will
accept it — but in the finished film that person is heard over a logo.

  Before you write a bite's Source in/out, look at the picture at those marks and
  confirm a person is on screen.

  - Thumbnails, a contact sheet, or just opening the file at that time — anything that
    shows you frames — is the only reliable check. These graphics are animated, so a
    "frozen picture" check does not find them, and their colour changes as they loop.
  - A broadcast file is often live for its first seconds and a card for the rest. If a
    file is 90 seconds long and the picture is a card from 15 seconds, then every bite
    taken after 15 seconds has no speaker on screen.
  - If the speaker cannot be shown, either quote them from a range where they can, or
    do not quote them. Do not let a name run over a graphic.

THE SAME GOES FOR THE OPENING
If the film opens on sound bites, those bites are the hook, and a hook on a card is
worse than no hook: the first thing a viewer sees is a station logo. Open on a face or
on live footage. The reader allows a bite at 00:00:00,000 with no narration before it;
that is how a hook is written.

ONE PERSON, ONE RANGE
A script may quote the same speaker in several bites, but each bite must name a range
where that speaker is visible. Two bites that both open on a logo are two bites with
nobody on screen, no matter whose name is on the caption.

CHECK THE PICTURE, THEN THE NUMBERS
The arithmetic checks in the app are exhaustive and will refuse a script whose numbers
do not add up. They cannot tell whether your footage was live at that moment. That is
the one check that stays yours, and it is the one that decides whether the finished
edit looks like television or like a slideshow.

REUSING A SHOT
A shot may come back later under different narration — that is normal documentary
practice. The app lists every repeated range and asks you to approve it before building.
It is a note, not an error. Do not repeat an *identical* range twice: showing the exact
same frames twice is refused, because it means the picture could not cover the words.

ON-SCREEN CAPTIONS
`On-screen caption:` is the literal text burned into the picture. Captions are on by
default for the project, so this line decides whether text appears in that beat.

  On-screen caption: Sunset Crossing Apartments      -> that text is burned in
  On-screen caption: None                            -> no caption on this beat

Write `None` (or leave the line out) wherever you do not want text — never leave it blank
after the colon while expecting a caption, and never put quotation marks around None.
If you want no burned-in captions anywhere, turn captions off for the whole project; the
SRT and the narration are unaffected by that setting.

WHAT THE STORYBOARD DOES WITH YOUR RANGES
It fits each beat's picture to its own narration clock and records every change:
shortens a range that is longer than the narration, extends one that is shorter inside
the same file, snaps OUT to a real frame. It never changes your script, never swaps in a
file you did not name, and never speeds up or repeats footage to cover a gap. If it
cannot fit a beat honestly, it stops and says which beat and why.

CHECK YOURSELF BEFORE YOU UPLOAD
The app will tell you all of this on upload, but fixing it here saves a round trip.

  1. Length of each file. Note each video's real duration. For every beat, Source out
     must be less than or equal to the length of the file named on its Full source line.
     This is the single mistake that stops a storyboard being built.
  2. Span arithmetic. For narration: Narration out − Narration in must equal
     Source out − Source in, and equal Master out − Master in. For a bite:
     Source out − Source in must equal the Duration you wrote.
  3. Narration clock. First beat starts at 0. Each bite's PAUSED time is the previous
     narration's OUT. Each RESUMES begins at the same PAUSED time. The final narration
     OUT equals your audio duration to within a quarter second.
  4. Master clock. Each beat's Master in is the previous beat's Master out, and the last
     Master out is your TOTAL MASTER RUNTIME.
  5. Runtime. Narration length plus the duration of every bite equals your TOTAL MASTER
     RUNTIME. Do not write the runtime you want; write the sum you get.
  6. Live picture. Look at the frames at each bite's Source in and Source out. A person
     must be on screen. A station card, an ident or an app promo is not a speaker, and
     the app cannot check this for you.
  7. Hook. If the film opens on bites, the first frames are a face or live footage.
  8. Table. Every block starts where the previous ended, block 1 starts at zero, and the
     last block ends at your audio duration.
  9. Captions. Every beat has an On-screen caption line: the literal text, or None.
 10. Photographs. Every person the narration names has a hold clip (see TEMPLATE F),
     every mention of them points at that clip, and no two mentions reuse the same
     source window. Every photograph is on a narration beat, never a sound bite.

If numbers 1 to 5 hold, the storyboard can be built and the only remaining work is
editorial review.

AFTER YOU UPLOAD
1. Upload the narration audio first, then the script (or the .srt), then every source.
2. Read the panel. Anything it lists here is a real conflict with your files.
3. Open "Review the Beat picture plan". Read the picture-range changes and the repeated
   footage list, then build.
4. Review the timeline, then check the pictures.

The app never edits your script, invents a source, or decides identity, chronology,
speaker or reuse rights. Those stay your review.

WORKED EXAMPLE — A SHORT BUT COMPLETE SCRIPT
================================================================================
TEMPLATE G — b-roll that comes from your own footage
================================================================================

The picture in this production is three things and nothing else:

  1. PHOTOGRAPHS of the people the narration names          (Template F)
  2. FOOTAGE of people speaking, and the scene the sentence is about
  3. B-ROLL — a place, an object or an atmosphere, cut from that same footage

B-roll is not stock and it is not a graphic. It is a window you lift out of a video
you already uploaded: the police cars, the street, the bayou, the playground, the
courtroom benches, the memorial. This template is the part that goes wrong silently,
because a wrong b-roll shot renders perfectly and nobody notices until they watch it.

STEP 1 — find out what is really inside each file.

Do not trust a shot list you were handed, and do not trust a filename. A file called
`S04_apartments.mp4` may open on a photograph with baked-in blur, cut to a reporter
talking to camera, and only reach the apartments at forty-six seconds. Open the file
and look at frames before you write a single Beat. Extract them without installing
anything:

    .venv\Scripts\python -c "import imageio_ffmpeg,subprocess as s;f=imageio_ffmpeg.get_ffmpeg_exe();[s.run([f,'-y','-loglevel','error','-ss',str(t),'-i','S04.mp4','-frames:v','1',f'f{t}.png']) for t in range(0,140,10)]"

Then open the PNGs. Five seconds apart is enough to find the cuts; one second apart
around a boundary you care about. Write down, for each usable stretch, what is on
screen and which second it starts and ends.

STEP 2 — record what you found, once.

`data/broll_catalog.json` holds one entry per usable window. Each entry says the file,
the in and out, the words that should bring it up, what is on screen, and what kind of
picture it is. The `kind` field matters more than the tags:

    place     a location or an object. Safe to cut to on the matching word.
    speaker   a person talking to camera. Only when that person is the subject.
    people    a named individual going about their business. Only on their name.
    graphic   a map, a lower third, a photo on a screen. Never b-roll.

    {"id": "B06", "source": "S10", "in": 36.0, "out": 41.5,
     "tags": ["search", "searcher", "water", "bayou"],
     "what": "Two searchers in red and yellow rain jackets on the bank",
     "kind": "place", "verified": true}

`verified: true` means a frame from that window was actually looked at. Leave it false
and the planner refuses the window rather than guessing, because an unchecked guess
about what is on screen is worse than leaving footage up.

STEP 3 — let the narration choose.

    python tools/place_broll.py --project projects/<id> --plan

The planner reads the SRT, scores every spoken cue against the catalog's words, and
throws away four kinds of cue before it matches anything:

  - a cue that names a person. That cue takes their photograph (Template F), not a
    place. Cutting to the bayou while the narrator says "Maliyah Bass" is a picture of
    nowhere while somebody's name is spoken.
  - a cue that is somebody speaking — "here is", "listen to", "is about to tell",
    "says", "described". That cue belongs to the speaker's face.
  - a cue whose two subjects score the same. "Texas EquuSearch and an air unit were
    searching" does not say whether to show the searchers or the sky. A coin toss here
    is how the wrong picture ends up behind the words.
